Aakha: Walking Guidance for Blind Users

Turns a phone camera into short spoken cues for blind and low-vision walkers, with no LLM anywhere in the safety loop.

Ankit Pokhrel
1 min read·2026·demo videosource

The problem

A white cane finds what's at your feet. It doesn't tell you a scooter is closing in, or that a zebra crossing is right in front of you. And "is it safe to step forward" is the wrong question to hand to a large language model that can be slow or wrong. Aakha keeps the safety loop to classic computer vision and small, fast models.

How it works

The phone is the camera and the speaker. A laptop runs the pipeline, and the phone connects over a Cloudflare HTTPS tunnel by scanning a QR code.

  • YOLO11n with ByteTrack tracks objects every frame. Only things whose ground contact falls inside a walking corridor matter, so a bus across the road is ignored.
  • Collision cues come from how fast a tracked box grows: a looming object becomes "closing on person".
  • Depth Anything V2 runs in the background for walls, and classic CV finds zebra crossings (edges and Hough lines) and traffic-light colours.
  • One voice. Every part of the system posts to one priority queue, and exactly one consumer speaks, so cues never talk over each other. A soft beep means the path ahead is clear.
  • Hold to talk: offline Vosk speech recognition handles "read this" (on-device OCR), "what am I holding" and "repeat that".

Designed to be used without sight

The whole phone screen is one button: tap to start or pause, hold to speak. There are no menus to find. Critical cues jump the queue but never cut off a sentence already playing, which can delay them a second or two, so every cue is kept short.
